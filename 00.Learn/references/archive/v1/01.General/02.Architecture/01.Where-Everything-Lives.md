# Where Does Everything Live in a CloudStack Cloud?

**Level:** Beginner → Intermediate · **Type:** Big picture · **Written against:** commit `1a48a87587` · **Reading time:** about 25 minutes

*Part 1 of 2 of [Architecture: One Head Office, Many Floors](README.md). [Part 2](02.Who-Calls-Whom.md) follows the line between the machines.*

---

Two servers arrive in your server room, with a note: "Build us a cloud."

You already have the picture from [post 01](../01.Overview.md#a-hotel-for-computers): a hotel chain run from one head office, with floors full of rooms. But before you install anything, you have practical questions.

Which software goes on which machine? Where does the second machine sit in CloudStack's building plan? Where will its rooms keep their disks? Which cables does it need?

This post answers them in that order, with your own two-machine lab as the example. You're the **operator** here: the person who builds and runs the cloud ([post 01](../01.Overview.md#who-sets-the-rules-developers-operators-and-users)). The people who later work in it are your cloud's users, the hotel's guests.

**What you'll learn:**

- Which machines a small CloudStack cloud runs on, what runs on each, and which doors (ports) the head office opens
- How a cloud is divided into regions, zones, pods, clusters and hosts, and what stops at each boundary
- Where VMs' disks and the room styles are kept: primary storage per host, cluster or zone, and secondary storage per zone
- The five kinds of traffic CloudStack keeps apart, and the three addresses of a system VM

---

## Two machines: a head office and a floor

A CloudStack cloud has two jobs to fill. The **head office** decides: it takes every request and writes everything down. The **floors** do the work: they run the VMs.

The docs put it this way: "most CloudStack deployments consist of the management server and the resources to be managed" ([Concepts and Terminology](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/concepts.html#deployment-architecture-overview)).

In your lab, each job gets a machine of its own:

![The two machines of the lab you'll build. Machine 2, a floor (a KVM host), holds the floor attendant (cloudstack-agent), the floor machinery (libvirt and KVM) and the rooms (your users' VMs, and the staff rooms, the system VMs). Machine 1, the head office's machine, holds the head office (the management server, package cloudstack-management), the ledger (MySQL) and the warehouse and storage room (NFS shares). A note says there are no lines between them yet: part 2 draws them.](../diagrams/02-lab.png)

*Click the picture to enlarge it.*

Machine 1 holds the head office, its ledger and the shared storage. Machine 2 is a floor: a KVM host, with its attendant, its machinery and its rooms.

Is two the minimum? No. "In its smallest deployment, a single machine can act as both the Management Server and the hypervisor host (using the KVM hypervisor)" ([Concepts and Terminology](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/concepts.html#deployment-architecture-overview)).

These are roles, not boxes:

- The [Quick Installation Guide](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/quickinstallationguide/qig.html) (QIG) puts a whole cloud on one machine: management server, MySQL, NFS file shares and KVM agent. **NFS** is a standard way to share folders over the network. The QIG warns: "This guide can NOT be used for production setup."
- Production spreads the roles out: "A production installation typically uses a separate NFS server" ([install guide](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/installguide/management-server/index.html)).
- [The lab we'll build](../11.Install-By-Hand/README.md) *(coming soon)* sits in between, like the docs' [small-scale deployment](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/choosing_deployment_architecture.html#small-scale-deployment): the management server with its database and NFS storage on one machine, and one KVM host.

> [!TIP]
> **OpenStack lens:** OpenStack's machines play four roles: controller, compute, storage and network nodes (the sister course's [The machines and their jobs](https://openstack.techphistudio.de/01.General/02.Architecture/#the-machines-and-their-jobs)). CloudStack needs two, and no network node: its routers are VMs on the floors ([part 2](02.Who-Calls-Whom.md#the-switchboard-reached-through-the-intercom)).

### The head office's machine

The head office is one package, `cloudstack-management`: [post 01's](../01.Overview.md#one-head-office-decides-everything) one Java program. Three things about its machine matter to you:

1. The ledger doesn't have to live on it.
2. It opens a few doors for others to come in.
3. One door people expect to find open is shut.

**Where's the ledger?** Wherever you put it. The head office finds its ledger through a settings file, `db.properties`, so the database can sit on the same machine or on one of its own.

Here are the two usual places, side by side, as settings in the management server's `db.properties` (the addresses are examples):

```text
# The ledger on the same machine (your lab)
db.cloud.host=localhost
db.cloud.port=3306
```

```text
# The ledger on its own machine (installs with several management servers)
db.cloud.host=192.168.42.20
db.cloud.port=3306
```

The install guide's rule: "When you are installing the management server on a single node, you can install the MySQL server locally. For an installation that has multiple management server nodes, we assume the MySQL database also runs on a separate node" ([Install the database server](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/installguide/management-server/index.html)). MySQL next to MariaDB is for [The Foundation Services](../05.Foundation-Services.md) *(coming soon)*.

**Its doors.** A **port** is the number that picks which program on a machine a connection reaches ([post 01](../01.Overview.md#guests-only-ever-talk-to-the-front-desk)). The head office answers on three ports and calls out on a fourth:

- 8080, the front desk: the API and the web UI,
- 8250, the **staff line** that floor attendants call ([part 2](02.Who-Calls-Whom.md#the-attendant-phones-in)),
- 9090, for other head offices, if there are any,
- 3306, out to the ledger.

The optional usage server, the billing office, goes on a management server's machine too ([Optional installation](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/installguide/optional_installation.html)).

**What about 8096?** It's a common misconception that 8096 is the admin API port. It's an *unauthenticated* API port, one that answers without checking who's asking, used by developer setups, and it's off unless someone switches it on. The docs are blunt: "The public Internet must not be able to access port 8096 or port 8250 on the Management Server" ([Security Requirements](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/network_setup.html#security-requirements)).

So installing the head office installs no ledger: the database is a separate install, on this machine or another.

    `db.properties` is made from this template ([code](https://github.com/apache/cloudstack/blob/1a48a87587c03472feefb081cfac71b2ebd0f407/client/conf/db.properties.in#L18-L30)):

    # in which the management server is running

    db.cloud.password=@DBPW@

    The unauthenticated port is a setting, `integration.api.port`, whose default is `0`, which means off; developer setups set it to 8096 ([code](https://github.com/apache/cloudstack/blob/1a48a87587c03472feefb081cfac71b2ebd0f407/server/src/main/java/com/cloud/api/ApiServer.java#L273-L279)):

### A floor's machine

A KVM floor gets one package, `cloudstack-agent`: the **floor attendant** ([post 01](../01.Overview.md#the-floors-do-the-work)), the program that carries out the head office's instructions on this host. Three things to know about it:

1. It needs the floor's machinery: it drives KVM through libvirt ([post 01](../01.Overview.md#the-magic-trick-virtualization)).
2. It gets back up by itself if it falls over.
3. It listens on no port. It talks to libvirt on its own machine, and dials out to the head office ([part 2](02.Who-Calls-Whom.md#the-attendant-phones-in)).

The first two are in its systemd unit, which you can read as [post 01 read the head office's](../01.Overview.md#one-head-office-decides-everything) ([code](https://github.com/apache/cloudstack/blob/1a48a87587c03472feefb081cfac71b2ebd0f407/packaging/systemd/cloudstack-agent.service#L22-L34)):

```text
# cloudstack: packaging/systemd/cloudstack-agent.service, lines 22–34
[Unit]
Description=CloudStack Agent
Documentation=http://www.cloudstack.org/
Requires=libvirtd.service
After=libvirtd.service

[Service]
Type=simple
EnvironmentFile=/etc/default/cloudstack-agent
ExecStartPre=/usr/share/cloudstack-common/scripts/installer/pre-check.sh
ExecStart=/usr/bin/java $JAVA_OPTS $JAVA_DEBUG -cp $CLASSPATH $JAVA_CLASS
Restart=always
RestartSec=10s
```

- `Requires=libvirtd.service`: no floor machinery, no attendant.
- `ExecStart=/usr/bin/java …`: one Java program again.
- `Restart=always` and `RestartSec=10s`: if the attendant falls over, systemd starts it again after 10 seconds.

And the third? The agent's code and the KVM plugin contain no listening server: nothing in them waits for a connection. That matters for your firewall, as [part 2's map](02.Who-Calls-Whom.md#who-talks-to-whom-the-whole-map) shows.

The docs add two rules for your floors: "the hypervisor host must not have any VMs already running", and "All hosts within a cluster must be homogeneous" (all alike) ([Minimum System Requirements](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/installguide/overview/index.html)).

??? info "Under the hood: which program, and as which user"

    The environment file that comes with the unit names the Java class it starts ([code](https://github.com/apache/cloudstack/blob/1a48a87587c03472feefb081cfac71b2ebd0f407/packaging/systemd/cloudstack-agent.default#L22)):

    ```text
    # cloudstack: packaging/systemd/cloudstack-agent.default, line 22
    JAVA_CLASS=com.cloud.agent.AgentShell
    ```

    Remember `AgentShell`: [part 2](02.Who-Calls-Whom.md#two-staff-rooms-with-their-own-phone) finds the same class inside two system VMs.

    The unit has no `User=` line, so systemd runs the agent as root, its default. The head office's unit, by contrast, says `User=cloud`. The code doesn't say why.

## A site divided into buildings, wings and floors

Your floor has its software. But where in the cloud does it *go*?

Every host gets an address in CloudStack's building plan: a site, a building and a wing. Each level has a job, and each is a boundary where something stops:

| Level | Hotel | Its job, and what stops at its edge |
|---|---|---|
| Region | A city | A whole install, with its own head office and ledger |
| Zone | A hotel site | The level your users pick, with its own warehouse, public addresses and network model |
| Pod | A building | One rack's wiring: one switch, one subnet |
| Cluster | A wing | Floors whose hosts run the same type of hypervisor and share the wing's storage rooms; live migration, and the neighbours' check on a quiet floor, stay inside |
| Host | A floor | One machine |

The docs put the nesting in one sentence: "Hosts are contained within clusters, clusters are contained within pods, pods are contained within zones, and zones can be contained within regions" ([Concepts and Terminology](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/concepts.html#about-hosts)). You fill it in from the outside: when you add a zone, the wizard asks for "the first pod, cluster, host, primary storage, and secondary storage" ([Adding a Zone](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/installguide/configuration.html#adding-a-zone)).

In your lab, there's one of each:

Inside the site is a building (pod 1), inside it a wing (cluster 1), inside it a floor (host 1, your KVM host), and on the floor a room (VM). A note says there's one of each level, and your floor's address is its zone, pod and cluster.](../diagrams/02-lab-plan.png)

### The building plan is in the ledger

Is the plan real, or just a drawing?

It's real. Each level is a table in the ledger, and each row names the row it sits inside, like a folder path: a host names its cluster, a cluster its pod, a pod its zone.

**Here the hotel breaks.** A room names its floor only while it runs. A VM's host column may be empty ([code](https://github.com/apache/cloudstack/blob/1a48a87587c03472feefb081cfac71b2ebd0f407/engine/schema/src/main/java/com/cloud/vm/VMInstanceVO.java#L107-L108)):

```java
// cloudstack: engine/schema/src/main/java/com/cloud/vm/VMInstanceVO.java, lines 107–108
@Column(name = "host_id", updatable = true, nullable = true)
protected Long hostId;
```

`nullable = true` lets `host_id` be empty, and it is whenever the VM is stopped. A stopped room has no floor at all, while its disks wait in the storage room.

### Zones: the sites your users choose

A zone is usually one data centre, built to fail on its own: "each zone can have its own power supply and network uplink", for "physical isolation and redundancy" ([About Zones](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/concepts.html#about-zones)).

It's also the level a user of your cloud [must pick](../01.Overview.md#why-your-users-pick-a-hotel-site-never-a-floor). And each zone has its own warehouse, its own public addresses ("Public IP addresses from different zones do not overlap", [Networking in a Zone](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/adminguide/networking_and_traffic.html)), and its own network model.

The network model is a choice you make once. `createZone` won't go without it ([code](https://github.com/apache/cloudstack/blob/1a48a87587c03472feefb081cfac71b2ebd0f407/api/src/main/java/org/apache/cloudstack/api/command/admin/zone/CreateZoneCmd.java#L78-L79)):

```java
// cloudstack: api/src/main/java/org/apache/cloudstack/api/command/admin/zone/CreateZoneCmd.java, lines 78–79
@Parameter(name = ApiConstants.NETWORK_TYPE, type = CommandType.STRING, required = true, description = "Network type of the zone, can be Basic or Advanced")
private String networkType;
```

`required = true` makes it compulsory, and it's for life: "A zone is either basic or advanced for its entire lifetime" ([Basic and Advanced Networking](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/network_setup.html#basic-and-advanced-networking)).

- **Basic**: one shared network, AWS-style, with guests kept apart by security groups (filtering by IP address).
- **Advanced**: each guest network isolated, usually on its own VLAN, a numbered virtual network ([below](#the-wiring-five-kinds-of-traffic)).

The QIG picks Advanced; [The Core Subsystems](../07.Core-Subsystems.md) *(coming soon)* compares the two. Since 4.18, a zone is also **Core**, the full kind, or **Edge**, a lightweight one without staff rooms, the system VMs ([part 2](02.Who-Calls-Whom.md#why-rooms-and-not-machines-of-their-own)). Who picks the floor within a site is for [How It Unfolds](../04.How-It-Unfolds.md) *(coming soon)*.

### Pods: buildings with their own wiring

A pod is a rack of servers with its own switch: one building's wiring. Your users never see it: "Pods are not visible to the end user" ([About Pods](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/concepts.html#about-pods)). So why have one?

For the wiring. "A pod often represents a single rack. Hosts in the same pod are in the same subnet." A **rack** is a tall cabinet of servers, and a pod "includes a layer-2 switch", a box connecting its machines directly ([Concepts and Terminology](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/concepts.html#cloud-infrastructure-overview)).

A **subnet** is a range of addresses on one local network, written like `192.168.42.0/24` (**CIDR** notation). Traffic for anywhere else goes to the subnet's **gateway**, a router; that's why the pod's row in the ledger remembers its gateway.

The pod also hands out a block of staff extension numbers. Two staff rooms, the porter and the window from [post 01](../01.Overview.md#the-floors-do-the-work), get their management addresses "in the CIDR of the pod that they are created in" ([System Reserved IP Addresses](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/concepts.html#system-reserved-ip-addresses)). Your lab has one pod, and that's fine.

A pod is also something you can hand to one customer. As root admin, you can **dedicate** it to a domain or an account, and then "Only Users in that domain or its subdomain may use the infrastructure" ([Dedicating Resources](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/adminguide/accounts.html#dedicating-resources-to-accounts-and-domains)).

### Clusters: wings of identical floors

A cluster is a wing of alike floors: hosts that all run the same type of hypervisor and share the wing's storage rooms, its primary storage. In the docs' words, "The hosts in a cluster all have identical hardware, run the same hypervisor, are on the same subnet, and access the same shared primary storage": the same *type* of hypervisor, since each host runs its own. A cluster "consists of one or more hosts and one or more primary storage servers" ([About Clusters](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/concepts.html#about-clusters)).

Four things stop at a wing's edge:

1. **One type of hypervisor.** Every host in a cluster runs the same type of hypervisor, say all KVM; each host still runs its own copy.
2. **Shared storage rooms**, which every floor of the wing can reach ([below](#primary-storage-three-sizes-of-storage-room)).
3. **Moving rooms.** **Live migration** moves a running VM to another host without stopping it ([post 01's note](../01.Overview.md#behind-the-staff-only-door)). By default, a room moves only between floors of its wing. The one way out: when the disks can move too, a room may go to another wing of the same building (see under the hood, below).
4. **Neighbours vouch for each other** when a floor goes quiet ([part 2](02.Who-Calls-Whom.md#when-a-floor-goes-quiet)).

Even with only local storage, "clusters are still required organizationally".

Your lab's wing has one floor, so two of these wait for a second host: there's nowhere to move a running room, and no neighbour to vouch for a quiet one.

??? info "Under the hood: one hypervisor type, and how far a running room may move"

    When CloudStack lists the hosts a running VM could suitably move to, it plans within the VM's cluster, or, if the disks can move too, its pod ([code](https://github.com/apache/cloudstack/blob/1a48a87587c03472feefb081cfac71b2ebd0f407/server/src/main/java/com/cloud/server/ManagementServerImpl.java#L1775-L1781)):

> [!TIP]
> **Kubernetes lens:** Kubernetes zones and regions are labels on nodes, such as `topology.kubernetes.io/zone` ("A zone represents a logical failure domain", [Well-Known Labels](https://kubernetes.io/docs/reference/labels-annotations-taints/)). A CloudStack zone is a row of its own, with its own warehouse and networks, and Kubernetes has no building or wing levels. Mind the word clashes: a CloudStack pod is a rack, a cluster a wing. (Nothing to do with CloudStack's Kubernetes Service, CKS.)

> [!TIP]
> **OpenStack lens:** OpenStack's availability zones "are not modeled in the database"; they're metadata on host aggregates, and "should not be assumed to map to fault domains" ([Availability Zones](https://docs.openstack.org/nova/latest/admin/availability-zones.html)). A CloudStack cluster is a strict group, not a tag, and there's no "cells" layer. Compare the sister course's [How clouds grow](https://openstack.techphistudio.de/01.General/02.Architecture/#how-clouds-grow-zones-cells-and-regions), whose "wings" are something else.

### Regions: each city is a separate install

Above the sites sits the city, the region. It isn't one more box in the same ledger: "Each region has its own CloudStack instance" ([Adding a Region](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/installguide/configuration.html#adding-a-region)). Another city means another head office and another ledger.

So a zone's row in the ledger names no region: the whole ledger belongs to one.

Your lab has one region. A fresh install creates it ([code](https://github.com/apache/cloudstack/blob/1a48a87587c03472feefb081cfac71b2ebd0f407/engine/schema/src/main/resources/META-INF/db/schema-40to410.sql#L265)):

```sql

INSERT INTO `cloud`.`region` values ('1','Local','http://localhost:8080/client/');
```

That's region 1, "Local", the `region.id=1` in [the head office's `db.properties`](#the-head-offices-machine) (under the hood). A second region is a second install, whose account, user and domain tables you copy "from the region 1 database". Another city, sharing a photocopied guest list.

## Where the disks live: storage rooms and the warehouse

Your floor will run rooms. Where do their disks live?

In two kinds of storage, with very different jobs:

- **Primary storage** holds running VMs' disks. It's "typically located close to the hosts for increased performance" ([About Primary Storage](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/concepts.html#about-primary-storage)): the storage rooms beside the rooms.
- **Secondary storage** holds the catalogue of room styles, templates and ISOs, plus snapshots, saved copies of a disk: one warehouse per site.

![A room (VM) keeps its disks in a storage room (primary storage), which holds running VMs' disks. The warehouse (secondary storage) holds room styles and snapshots, and a room style is copied from it into the storage room. A note says there's one warehouse per site, and storage rooms beside the rooms.](../diagrams/02-storage.png)

*Click the picture to enlarge it.*

A room keeps its disks in a storage room, and a new room's style is copied in from the warehouse. As in the building plan, long-dashed means "keeps its data in" and short-dotted "copied in".

### Primary storage: three sizes of storage room

A storage room's size is *who can reach it*: one floor, one wing or the whole site.

It's either **shared**, reached over the network by every floor of its wing or site, or **local**, a disk inside one floor. The usual shared kind is NFS (Network File System), which shares a folder from one Linux machine to others.

| Scope | Hotel | Who can use it | Pick it when | Caveats |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cluster (the default) | A wing's storage room | the hosts of that cluster | the usual case; the QIG picks it | another cluster's VMs reach the data only through a copy via the warehouse |
| Zone | A storage room for the whole site | that hypervisor's hosts, in every cluster of the zone | VMs must move between wings without copies | KVM and VMware, not XenServer |
| Host (local) | A cupboard on one floor | that host only | you have no shared storage | not shared: no live migration without moving the disks, and no restart on another floor if this one dies |

Why a whole-site storage room? To avoid copies. With cluster-wide storage, data another wing needs "must be copied from one cluster to another, using the zone's secondary storage as an intermediate step. This operation can be unnecessarily time-consuming" ([About Primary Storage](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/concepts.html#about-primary-storage)).

Why not always a cupboard? Because no other floor can reach it. A cluster's hosts "access the same shared primary storage", so it follows that a VM on local storage can't live-migrate unless its disks move too.

For a small lab like yours, the QIG's wizard step, asking for the scope, cluster or zone, says it all: "Scope - We'll use `Cluster` even though either is fine in this case."

### Secondary storage: one warehouse per site

The warehouse holds templates, ISOs and snapshots: "A zone-wide resource" ([Concepts and Terminology](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/concepts.html#cloud-infrastructure-overview)), usually on NFS.

Who goes in? It's a common misconception that only the porter, the secondary storage VM, does. "In addition to the hosts, CloudStack's Secondary Storage VM mounts and writes to secondary storage" ([Secondary Storage VM](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/adminguide/systemvm.html#secondary-storage-vm)). KVM hosts fetch room styles themselves, to copy into their storage room, so your NFS share must let them in too.

The warehouse can also be **object storage**, S3 or Swift, reached by web requests. Then it serves the whole cloud, with an NFS "staging" store per zone ([About Secondary Storage](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/concepts.html#about-secondary-storage)):

| Warehouse | Serves | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| NFS (the default; the QIG and your lab) | one zone | the porter and the KVM hosts mount it |
| S3 or Swift, plus NFS staging per zone | the whole cloud | "Heterogeneous Secondary Storage is not supported in Regions" |

Why NFS, and what else could serve, is for [The Foundation Services](../05.Foundation-Services.md) *(coming soon)*.

## The wiring: five kinds of traffic

Your floor needs cables. Which ones?

CloudStack names five kinds of traffic, each with its own way through the building: the guests' corridors, the street, the staff passages, the goods lift to the warehouse, and an intercom. Each kind can get its own cable, or several can share one: "Each physical network can carry one or more types of network traffic" ([About Physical Networks](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/concepts.html#about-physical-networks)).

First, some words:

- **NIC**: a network interface card, a machine's network port.
- **VLAN**: one physical network split into several numbered virtual ones.
- **Bridge**: a virtual switch inside a Linux host that VMs plug into, such as `cloudbr0`.
- **Physical network**: a zone's real wiring, matched to a NIC on every host.
- **Traffic type**: what a network carries.

Here are the five ([Traffic Types](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/concepts.html#advanced-zone-network-traffic-types)):

| Traffic type | Hotel | Carries | On a KVM host |
|---|---|---|---|
| Guest | The guest corridors | the VMs' own traffic | `cloudbr1`, in the KVM guide's layout |
| Public | The street | traffic to and from the outside world, through the virtual router | `cloudbr1` too |
| Management | The staff passages | the head office ↔ hosts and system VMs; the staff line runs here | `cloudbr0` |
| Storage | The goods lift to the warehouse | the porter ↔ secondary storage, if you add a storage network | a separate NIC |
| Control | The intercom | a host ↔ its own system VMs, on link-local addresses, 169.254.x.x | its own bridge, `cloud0` |

**Careful with "storage".** The docs disagree on whether primary-storage traffic uses it (under the hood, below); your lab has no storage network, so this can wait for [The Core Subsystems](../07.Core-Subsystems.md) *(coming soon)*.

**Why `169.254`?** Those are **link-local** addresses, which work only on one network segment. They "in theory provide more than 65,000 private IP addresses", sparing the scarce management ones ([System Reserved IP Addresses](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/concepts.html#system-reserved-ip-addresses)). VMware routers get a management address instead ([Choosing a Hypervisor](https://docs.cloudstack.apache.org/en/4.22.1.1/conceptsandterminology/choosing_deployment_architecture.html#choosing-a-hypervisor)).

The install guides cable a KVM host with one bridge or with two; your lab's layout is for [the install posts](../11.Install-By-Hand/README.md) *(coming soon)* to settle.

A staff room, a system VM, usually gets three addresses, shown as columns in the web UI's System VMs list:

| Column in the System VMs list | Which door |
|---|---|
| `publicip` | the door onto the street |
| `privateip` | the door onto the staff passages: "private" is CloudStack's word for management here |
| `linklocalip` | the door onto the intercom |

## Recap: placing one floor

1. **Installed:** `cloudstack-agent`, the attendant, beside libvirt on machine 2; the head office, its ledger and the NFS shares on machine 1.
2. **Placed:** in a zone, a pod and a cluster, each a row in the ledger, inside region 1.
3. **Given storage:** disks in a storage room of the wing or the site, or in a cupboard; room styles from the zone's warehouse.
4. **Cabled:** staff passages, street and guest corridors, plus the `cloud0` intercom.

Your floor is ready. But the head office doesn't know it's there yet: [part 2](02.Who-Calls-Whom.md) follows the call that changes that.

## The one-paragraph version

A CloudStack cloud runs on two kinds of machine: the head office's, with the management server and, in small clouds, its ledger (MySQL) and NFS storage; and the floors, hypervisor hosts that run the VMs, each with an attendant, `cloudstack-agent`, on KVM. Every floor has an address: a zone (a site: what users pick, with its own warehouse, public addresses and network model), a pod (a building: a rack, a switch, a subnet) and a cluster (a wing: hosts with the same type of hypervisor and shared storage rooms, the live-migration boundary), each a row in the ledger; a region is a whole separate install. Running disks live in storage rooms per floor, wing or site (primary storage); room styles and snapshots in the zone's warehouse (secondary storage). Five kinds of traffic keep guests, the street, the staff, the warehouse and the floor's intercom apart.
